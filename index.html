    Spending crypto with a card

    Merchants almost never accept crypto directly. A crypto-linked card lets you spend crypto anywhere cards are accepted, while the merchant still gets paid in dollars. The trick happens behind the scenes: at the moment you tap, something has to turn your crypto into money the card network understands.

    The core problem: card payments must be approved in a couple of seconds and usually settle in dollars on business days. Crypto lives on blockchains that run on their own clocks, with prices that move by the minute. Every crypto card design is a different answer to bridging those two worlds.

    Three ways crypto cards are funded

    Model 1

    Convert at the moment you pay

    You hold crypto like BTC or ETH. When you tap, the app sells just enough at the current price to cover the purchase.

    1. Card authorization reaches the app by webhook
    2. App quotes the price and reserves the crypto
    3. App sells it for dollars and prefunds the BIN sponsor

    Simple for users, but every purchase is a crypto sale, with price risk and possible taxes.

    Model 2

    Spend a stablecoin balance

    You hold a dollar-pegged stablecoin like USDC. Since 1 USDC is designed to equal $1, there's no price to quote at the register.

    1. App checks the USDC balance and reserves the amount
    2. Card is approved like a normal debit card
    3. Settlement happens in dollars or, in some programs, directly in USDC

    A popular design for newer cards because it removes price swings from checkout.

    Model 3

    Borrow against your crypto

    Your crypto stays put as collateral, and the card draws on a credit line backed by it. You repay later in crypto or dollars.

    1. App locks crypto as collateral
    2. Purchases draw on a credit line
    3. If prices fall, you may need to add collateral

    Avoids selling crypto at the register, but adds lending rules and liquidation risk.

    The crypto card stack

    A crypto card is several companies working together. Here's what each layer does, top to bottom.

    Crypto app

    Program manager. Owns the customer, the wallet and the card design. Decides in real time whether to approve each purchase.

    Crypto card platform

    Specialists that bundle issuing, compliance and crypto conversion so apps can launch faster. Rain, Bridge (owned by Stripe) and Kulipa are examples.

    Issuer processor

    Runs the card: approvals, limits and the webhook that asks the app for a decision.

    BIN sponsor bank

    The licensed issuer. Lends its card network membership, runs KYC and anti-money-laundering checks, and holds the settlement funds.

    Card network

    Visa, Mastercard and others. Connects the card to merchants worldwide and settles between banks, now including stablecoin settlement options for select partners.

    Settling in stablecoins

    Normally the BIN sponsor pays the card network in dollars, and traditional settlement runs on business days only. That means a crypto app has to sell crypto and wire dollars to keep the account funded. Visa and Mastercard now let select issuers and acquirers settle in regulated stablecoins such as USDC instead. Stablecoins move over blockchains, including on weekends and holidays when bank wires don't.

    MonTueWedThuFriSatSun Bank wire Stablecoin

    Why it matters: fewer conversions mean lower costs and less cash tied up waiting for bank hours. It's one of the clearest places where blockchains are improving traditional card payments.

    What makes crypto cards hard

    Compliance

    The bank sponsor must verify every user (KYC) and monitor for money laundering. Crypto programs get extra scrutiny, which slows onboarding.

    Price swings

    If an app quotes ETH at one price and sells a minute later at another, someone eats the difference. Stablecoins mostly remove this.

    Taxes

    In the U.S., spending crypto like BTC or ETH generally counts as selling it, so each purchase can create a taxable gain or loss.

    Speed

    Card approvals take about 2 seconds; many blockchains take longer to confirm. Apps keep an off-chain ledger so they can answer in time.
